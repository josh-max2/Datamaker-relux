Understanding FDD Item 19

Item 19 is the section of a Franchise Disclosure Document where franchisors disclose actual financial performance of their existing outlets — revenue, costs, profit. It's also the most-misread, most-cherry-picked section of any FDD. This guide explains what's there, what isn't, and how to read what is.

What Item 19 is

Item 19 is one of 23 required sections in every Franchise Disclosure Document. Its formal name is "Financial Performance Representations" — abbreviated FPR. It's the only place in the entire FDD where the franchisor can legally tell you what their existing outlets actually earn.

That last sentence does a lot of work. Per FTC §436.5(s) — the federal franchise rule — only the franchisor's own Item 19 is a legally-authoritative source for any forecast or projection about your potential earnings. A franchise consultant, broker, or third-party analyst (including this site) cannot legally project what your specific outlet will earn. We can describe what franchisors disclosed; we can't tell you what you'll make.

The most important thing to know: Item 19 is optional

Franchisors are required to have an Item 19 section in the FDD. They are not required to put any numbers in it. If a franchisor doesn't want to disclose financial performance, Item 19 simply says: "We do not make any representations about a franchisee's future financial performance or the past financial performance of company-owned or franchised outlets."

That's a legally-protected non-statement. Roughly 30-40% of FDDs we've extracted include no Item 19 numbers at all. Brands with strong financial performance generally disclose; brands with mixed performance generally don't.

Buyer rule: "We don't disclose Item 19" is itself a meaningful signal. It doesn't mean the franchise is bad. It does mean you'll have to validate earnings through direct conversations with current franchisees (whose names + contact info the FDD must provide in Item 20).

What disclosed Item 19s contain

When franchisors do disclose, they typically include some combination of:

  • Gross sales / total revenue — top-line, by outlet or by cohort
  • Gross profit — revenue minus cost of goods sold
  • Net profit / EBITDA — much rarer; many FDDs stop at gross sales
  • Margin percentages — gross margin %, net margin %
  • Distribution statistics — average, median, top quartile (P75), bottom quartile (P25), highest, lowest

Watch for what's missing. Many FDDs disclose only gross sales without any cost or profit data. That's enough to know what outlets sell, but not whether they're profitable. A $1M gross-sales outlet running on 4% margin makes $40K profit; the same outlet on 18% margin makes $180K. The FDD might tell you one of those numbers, both, or neither.

Cohorts: subsets of outlets used for reporting

A cohort is a subset of outlets the franchisor groups together for reporting. The same FDD might report different financial figures for different cohorts:

  • All franchised outlets — the most inclusive cohort, usually most representative of typical buyer experience
  • Outlets open 1+ year / 2+ years / 3+ years — tenure-based; the franchisor's argument that earlier-stage outlets distort the average
  • Top 10% / Top 25% — performance-anchored; selectively showing best performers
  • Same-store outlets — only outlets open the full reporting period (excludes openings + closures)
  • Company-owned outlets — owned by the franchisor directly; usually higher revenue (better-staffed, prime locations) and not directly comparable to franchisee outlets

Always check the cohort label before quoting a number. "$612,000 average revenue" means very different things if it's an all-franchised figure vs. a top-25%-of-company-owned figure.

How to read the distribution

Averages can be deeply misleading in franchise data because outlet performance follows a power-law distribution: a handful of top performers can pull the average up sharply while most outlets earn much less. Look for these distribution markers:

  • Median — the middle outlet. Less skewed by top performers than the mean. Usually the most representative single number.
  • P25 / P75 (first and third quartiles) — 25% of outlets earned less than P25, 25% earned more than P75. The spread tells you variance.
  • "% of outlets above average" — when this number is well below 50%, the distribution is right-skewed (a few high earners drag the mean up). Below 40% is a strong skew signal.

If a franchisor reports only "average" without median or quartile data, treat the number with extra skepticism. Ask current franchisees during validation calls.

How buyers should use Item 19

  1. Identify the cohort. Is this all franchisees, or a filtered subset?
  2. Look at the median, not just the average. Or at minimum the P25 and P75.
  3. Compute the implied profit. Gross sales × industry-typical margin = rough net. Be conservative on the margin.
  4. Compare to the investment. Item 7 tells you the total cost. Item 19 implies the return. The ratio is your breakeven horizon.
  5. Validate with current franchisees. Item 20 of the FDD must list every existing franchisee's name, location, and phone number. Call them. Ask their actual numbers. Item 19 is the franchisor's framing; franchisee conversations are reality.

Red flags in Item 19

  • "Top X%" cohorts without an all-franchised baseline — selective reporting.
  • Only gross sales, no profit information — you can't tell if the outlets are profitable.
  • Very small sample size — fewer than 10 outlets in a cohort = high noise.
  • Outlet count doesn't match Item 20 — franchisor cherry-picked which outlets to include. Subtract Item 19 cohort size from Item 20 total to see how many were excluded.
  • Reporting period of less than 12 months — seasonality distortion.
  • Heavy footnotes shrinking the cohort definition — "outlets open more than X months that submitted complete data and weren't in a transition period…" usually means the franchisor is showing only their cleanest data.
